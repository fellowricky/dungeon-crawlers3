A dramatic boss-reveal portrait of a single undead soldier standing at attention in a dark underground stone chamber, seen from slightly below. Exactly one figure is in the scene.

She is Captain Vane, a woman, and she is a wight — long dead but still standing her post. Her build is a lean, upright, disciplined soldier's frame. Her face and hands are gaunt grey-white desiccated flesh drawn tight over bone, sunken cheeks, lips shrunk back from the teeth, skin cracked and papery like old vellum. Her hair is dry, colourless and pulled back severely into a soldier's braid. Her eye sockets are deep hollows holding two small, cold, steady points of pale blue-white light — no pupils, no warmth, just a level, patient, terribly aware stare directed straight at the viewer. Her expression is not feral or snarling. It is composed, disciplined and quietly implacable, the face of an officer waiting to be relieved.

Her kit is the whole point of the picture, and it is immaculate. It is strictly MEDIEVAL fantasy soldier's equipment, of a kind that would suit a knight or a castle garrison. She wears a riveted steel mail hauberk over a quilted padded gambeson, a sleeveless cloth surcoat in faded dark blue-green hanging to mid-thigh, a wide studded leather sword-belt with a heavy iron buckle, a leather baldric across the body, mail-backed leather gauntlets, and worn leather boots with cross-gartered strapping. Over the surcoat she wears a captain's sash of dulled crimson, knotted at the hip. Her rank is shown only by a simple abstract geometric metal badge pinned at the shoulder — an abstract shape only, no letters, no words, no writing anywhere.

Her clothing must NOT look modern or post-medieval in any way. There is no long tailored greatcoat, no frock coat, no trench coat, no lapels, no shoulder boards, no epaulettes, no gold aiguillette or braided cord loops, no rows of brass buttons, no peaked cap, no cravat, no military dress uniform of the Napoleonic, Victorian, Prussian or First World War kind. Nothing about the outfit should suggest a modern army officer.

The mail and surcoat are old, faded and worn thin, but maintained with obsessive care: the mail oiled and free of rust, the surcoat brushed and mended, buckles polished, straps squared, not a strap out of place. The contrast between the immaculate kit and the corpse wearing it is the point.

She stands rigidly at attention, shoulders square, chin level, one grey hand resting on the pommel of a sheathed longsword, the other at her side. A dented steel helm is held correctly under one arm.

Behind her the chamber is ancient dressed stone, far older and finer than the tower above — huge precisely fitted blocks, a low vaulted ceiling, a dark stairway descending steeply away into blackness behind her. Faintly visible in the shadows to either side are neat rows of folded kit and stacked equipment laid out with parade precision along the walls. Everything is orderly. Nothing is disturbed.

Lighting is cold and low: pale blue-white light rising faintly from the stair below and from her eyes, plus one weak shaft of grey daylight from far above catching her shoulders and the edge of her sash. Deep black shadow, drifting dust, breath-cold air. Palette of cold blue-grey, dead white, faded uniform blue-green and one muted crimson accent. Mood of terrible discipline — a soldier who was never told she could stop.
